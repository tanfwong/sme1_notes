# Introduction

## What is *random*?

- Some behavior that we cannot perfectly predict, as opposed to being *deterministic*.

## Why random?

- **Theologian:** We can't know God's mind!
- **Copenhagen quantum physicist:** The laws of quantum physics are fundamentally random.

## What to do with random?

- Although we cannot perfectly determine random behavior, we still want to obtain its specifications to some degree.
- **Probability Theory** is the theory that systematically studies and characterizes randomness.
- In this course, we will take an engineering approach to Probability Theory—more operational and less concerned with mathematical rigor.

## How to study probability?

1. **Intuition**

   - ***Example:*** Dr. Wong is a nice guy; he will probably give me a good grade in EEE 5544.
   - This approach is not systematic, relies on guessing, and cannot
     quantify the "likelihood" of the behavior.

2. **Relative frequency (a.k.a. counting)**

   - ***Example:*** Dr. Wong taught EEE 5544 three times in the past. Altogether, $200$ students took Dr. Wong's classes, and $50$ of them received an $A$. Thus, the chance of receiving an $A$ in Dr. Wong's class is

     $$
     \frac{50}{200}=\frac{1}{4}.
     $$

   - This assumes that every grade is “equally likely,” which may not be the case.
   - What if there are infinitely many possibilities?

3. **Axiomatic theory**

   - Developed by Kolmogorov in the early 1900s.
   - Identify a few basic axioms that fit intuition.
   - Build a mathematically consistent system based on the axioms.
   - Use measure theory to construct the mathematical system.
   - This is the approach that we will follow.
